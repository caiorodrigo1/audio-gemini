Standing in the parking lot of Saint Catherine's Cathedral, I watched my son's wedding guests file through the ornate doors like ants marching toward honey. Mercedes, BMWs, and Teslas lined the circular drive—not a single pickup truck or modest sedan in sight. Forty-three years I'd raised that boy, and here I was, uninvited to his wedding, clutching a manila envelope that would change everything.
The morning had started like any other. I'd been nursing my coffee on the porch when Mrs. Henderson from next door came rushing over, her face flushed with that particular look women get when they're about to deliver devastating news disguised as helpfulness.
"Frank, honey, I saw the wedding announcement in the society pages," she'd said, wringing her hands. "Your Michael's marrying that Wellington girl today."
The Wellington girl. Victoria Wellington, daughter of Richard Wellington, the real estate mogul who owned half the commercial district downtown. I'd heard about her through the grapevine—Princeton educated, trust fund baby, the kind of woman who'd never set foot in a place like Tony's Diner where Michael and I used to share pancakes every Saturday morning.
"I know about the wedding, Mrs. Henderson," I'd lied, because admitting your own son hadn't bothered to invite you to the most important day of his life felt like swallowing glass.
But I hadn't known. Three months since Michael last called, six months since he'd visited. The distance between us had grown like a cancer, starting small after he'd graduated from business school and taken that job at Wellington Properties. Suddenly, our weekly dinners became monthly phone calls, which became holiday texts, which became nothing at all.
I'd tried reaching out. Left voicemails that went unreturned. Sent Christmas cards that weren't acknowledged. Even drove to his fancy downtown apartment once, only to have the doorman tell me Mr. Patterson—he'd started using his mother's maiden name instead of mine—wasn't accepting visitors.
My own son, treating me like some kind of vagrant.
The envelope in my hands contained twenty years of secrets, documents I'd hoped never to use. But watching those wedding guests—people who'd known Michael for maybe two years—celebrate while his father stood excluded in a parking lot, I realized some truths couldn't stay buried forever.
I'd worn my best suit, the navy blue one Sarah had bought me for our twentieth anniversary. She'd been gone eight years now, and I still missed her voice in moments like this. She would have known what to say, how to bridge the gap between Michael and me. But Sarah was gone, and I was here alone, about to crash my son's wedding with revelations that would shake the very foundation of his new life.
The church bells chimed, announcing the ceremony's start. Through the stained glass windows, I could see shadows moving—guests taking their seats, the wedding party assembling. Michael would be up there soon, promising to love and honor Victoria Wellington until death do them part, just like I'd promised his mother all those years ago.
But some promises get tested in ways you never expect.
I tucked the envelope inside my jacket and walked toward the cathedral doors. The security guard—because of course they had security—looked me up and down with professional skepticism.
"Excuse me, sir. Are you on the guest list?"
"I'm the groom's father," I said, surprised by how steady my voice sounded.
He consulted his clipboard, frowning. "I don't see a Frank Patterson here."
"Try Frank Mitchell," I said, using Sarah's maiden name. "Sometimes there are mix-ups with the invitations."
Another frown, another scan of the list. "Sir, I'm going to have to ask you to—"
"Check with the bride's family," I interrupted. "Richard Wellington knows me. We have business to discuss."
That wasn't entirely true, but it wasn't entirely false either. Wellington and I had history, though Michael didn't know it. Nobody knew it except the two of us, and that secret had been eating at me for twenty years.
The guard spoke into his radio, got a response, and stepped aside. "Go ahead, sir. But please keep it quiet. The ceremony's about to begin."
